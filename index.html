Current direction

What happens after the technology works

After years developing technology, I got curious about the questions around it — who owns the problem, who pays for it, and why good technology stalls after R&D.

Defense & dual-use technology

My background is Navy- and Army-sponsored aircraft R&D. I’m now looking at where complex physical systems, autonomy, and software meet real-world deployment.

Autonomous & complex physical systems

I’m increasingly interested in autonomous platforms — extending the modeling, simulation, and optimization work I’ve done on rotorcraft and morphing aircraft in that direction.

Technology transition & adoption

At Berkeley, I’ve done structured customer discovery through NSF I-Corps — testing assumptions against real customers before deciding what to build, the same habit I carried from years of proposal work.

Background

Professional trajectory

My path started in aerospace engineering: a PhD and master’s at the University of Michigan, including Office of Naval Research–supported research on helicopter shipboard landings, and a short stint at an eVTOL startup building its flight-simulation strategy from the ground up. For the last six years, I’ve led government-funded R&D and business development at Continuum Dynamics — winning ~$1.45M in NASA and Army funding, taking an Army STTR from Phase I to Phase II, and opening new commercial clients in advanced air mobility. I’m now at Berkeley Haas, building out the rest of the toolkit: strategy, commercialization, and how organizations adopt new technology.
